A practical guide · Software Inc (Beta 1.8)

So you're running a software company.

This is the version of the guide you can actually play from. It won't explain the maths behind the game — it will tell you what to do next, what to spend money on, and which habits quietly destroy new studios.

Read time ~60 minutes Applies to any difficulty, any start year Assumes you've launched the game once

If you only read one thing

Six habits do most of the work in this game. Everything else is detail.

  1. Design properly. Three full passes through the design document before you start building. Skip it and the product is capped at "mediocre" no matter how long you work on it.
  2. Finish what you started. A finished simple feature beats a half-finished fancy one. Every time.
  3. Hire one really good programmer before you enter beta. Bug fixing is limited by your best programmer, not by how many people you throw at it.
  4. Price near the suggested price. Not below it to "sell more", not above it to "make more".
  5. Time your releases for autumn/winter. A November release sells roughly a third more than an August one.
  6. Keep the tail alive. Updates, sequels and add-ons are where the real money is; the launch week is only the beginning.

01Start here: your first two hours

If you've just made a company and you're staring at an empty office, do this. It's a safe, boring, profitable first project — exactly what you want while you learn the interface.

Before the steps Play the in-game tutorial first if you haven't — it teaches the interface in about twenty minutes, and this guide assumes you know roughly where things are. And if this is your very first company, pick Beginner or Easy: design moves faster, reputation matters less, and mistakes are survivable. Start a harder company later, once the rhythm makes sense.
  1. Pick a small, familiar software type. A 2D Editor, Audio Tool or Office Software is ideal: short development, one category, easy to understand. Save Games, Operating Systems and anything "hardware" for later — they are long, expensive and unforgiving.
  2. Hire a tiny team, not a big one. For a first project you want roughly one designer, two or three programmers and one or two artists, plus a lead designer. More people than the project needs actually slows you down and costs more.
  3. Make the design document and actually use it. Pick features you can afford in time. Aim for a design that takes about the same time as the game suggests (the "optimal" dev time shown for the type). You do not need every feature — you need enough features in the right areas.
  4. Run the design three times. When the design finishes, don't hit "start development" — run another design pass. Three passes is the difference between a product that can score well and one that cannot. (The design phase is fast; it is the cheapest quality you will ever buy.)
  5. Start alpha and watch the bars, not the clock. Progress should crawl up steadily. If one bar is stuck, you're missing that role — usually artists or programmers.
  6. Do one review, then one iteration, if you can afford it. A review costs money but shows you where you are; an iteration raises the quality ceiling and cuts bugs a little. Do this before beta.
  7. Enter beta and fix bugs until the curve flattens. Don't wait for zero bugs — wait until the remaining bug count stops dropping fast. Then release.
  8. Set the price to roughly the suggested value. If you're unsure, price slightly under it. Never price above 1.75× the suggestion — at that point people simply stop buying.
  9. Market it before release, not after. A press release and a press build in the months before launch are worth far more than the same money spent after.
  10. Release in autumn if you can. If your product is ready in summer, it is usually worth waiting a couple of months. November and December are the strongest months of the year.
A good first goal Don't aim for a 5-star hit. Aim for a finished, fairly priced, well-marketed product that makes a profit. Reputation and fans build slowly; the first game is about not going bankrupt and learning the rhythm.
The classic first-game trap Building a giant game with a tiny team and no design passes. It takes a year, ships unfinished, scores badly, and you can't afford the next one. Small, finished, on time — always.

What the game is actually asking of you

Software Inc is a juggling act between four things, and they fight each other:

Time

How long you develop

Longer means more features and more polish — and more salaries, more bugs, and a product that may be outdated when it ships. There is a sweet spot, and the game shows it to you as the type's "optimal" development time.

Quality

How good it is

Driven mostly by design passes and by actually finishing work, then by how good your people are. Quality is what makes reviews, reputation and long-term sales.

Money

What you can afford

Salaries are monthly and relentless. Contracts and small products keep you alive while you build the thing you actually want to build.

Attention

Whether anyone notices

Marketing, hype, followers and reputation decide whether a good product sells like a good product or disappears without a trace.

02The loop: a product from idea to money

Every product in the game — from a $20 tool to a console operating system — goes through the same four phases. Once you can recognise which phase you're in, the game stops feeling chaotic.

Phase 1

Design

Pick features, set the quality ceiling, choose your lead designer.

Phase 2

Alpha

Build it. Programmers write code, artists make art. Bugs pile up.

Phase 3

Beta

Fix bugs. Decide when "good enough" is good enough.

Phase 4

Release

Price it, market it, ship it, support it, sequel it.

Design: where the product is really decided

This is the part new players rush and later regret. The design document decides two things that you cannot change later: which features the product will have, and how good it is allowed to become.

Every feature has a star rating. One-star features are cheap and give a little value; two-star features cost similar time but give twice the value. Three-star features are special effects — they don't add "quality points" at all, but they do things (extra income per user, converting sales, and so on). So when you're choosing:

  • Prefer two-star features when you can afford them. They are the best value per month of work in the game.
  • Spread your features across the product's three "areas" (for a game: Gameplay, Graphics, Story). A product that is amazing in one area and empty in another scores worse than a balanced one.
  • Don't take everything. Once your feature list is roughly "the size the type expects", extra features buy you very little and cost you months and bugs.
  • Take three-star features deliberately — for their effect, not for their score.
Rule of thumb Three design passes minimum, four if you can afford the time. The first pass is rough, the second is decent, the third unlocks the product's full potential, and the fourth removes the randomness so you know what you're getting.
Why does re-running the design do anything?

Each pass through the design is a full review of the concept by your lead designer. With one pass, the team ends up with a vague brief and builds something "about 50 % as good as it could have been". Each extra pass raises the ceiling. After three passes the ceiling is as high as it goes; the fourth pass mainly makes the outcome predictable instead of a coin flip.

Frameworks: build once, reuse forever

A framework is a reusable technical base — an engine plus a set of features and tech levels that later products can borrow. You choose to create one in the design window, and it changes the maths of every product that follows.

Effect on developmentWhy it matters
Create a new framework+25 % time You pay once, on the product that builds it.
Build on your framework−25 % time (max) Every feature in the shared set is cheaper to implement.
Others use your frameworkup to 15 % royalty Passive income from other studios' products.
You use someone else'sroyalty you pay Fast, but a permanent cut of sales.
  • Best for series. If you plan three games in the same category, build the framework on the first and every sequel gets faster.
  • Frameworks can be updated to stay current — a stale framework gives less of a boost.
  • A framework is an asset: other companies licensing it is a real income stream, and it raises your standing as a technology company.
When to build one Build a framework when you're committing to a category for several products. Don't build one for a single release — you'd pay the 25 % and never use the discount.

Alpha: building it

Alpha is the long middle. Two bars matter: code and art. Your programmers fill the code bar, your artists fill the art bar, and features finish one by one.

If progress feels slow

  • Wrong roles: the most common cause. Check the feature list — if it's mostly art and you have one artist, that's your bottleneck.
  • Too many people: beyond the ideal team size, extra staff add less than they cost. The game shows the ideal team size per phase; follow it.
  • Bad tools/computers or a miserable office: unhappy staff work slower. Comfort, equipment and a decent room matter more than they look.

What to do while alpha runs

  • Line up your next design so teams aren't idle when this one ends.
  • Take a contract if cash is tight — but don't steal your best people from the main project for too long.
  • Watch the bug counter. A team of juniors generates a frightening number of bugs.
  • Start marketing in the last third of development, not at the start.

Beta: fixing bugs, and the one hire that saves you

Beta is short and brutal. Your team fixes bugs, and how many you can fix is limited by a single thing: the skill of your best programmer on the team. Not the number of programmers — the best one.

The single most important hire in the game Before beta, make sure you have at least one genuinely strong programmer assigned to the project. If your best programmer is mediocre, you will ship with a pile of bugs no matter how many people you assign, and bugs hit reviews, refunds and long-term sales.

When do you stop fixing and ship? Watch the bug count. Early in beta it drops quickly; later each fix takes longer. When the curve flattens and you still have a large pile, more beta time is usually better spent on marketing or on starting the next product.

Release: the part everyone gets wrong

Release is not one decision, it's four: when, at what price, with how much marketing, and what you do afterwards. Each has a section below. The short version:

  • When: late in the year is better; if the product is ready in August, consider waiting.
  • Price: at or just under the suggested price; never far above it.
  • Marketing: concentrated before launch, not spread thinly afterwards.
  • Afterwards: patches, updates and add-ons keep the product earning for years.

03People: the only resource that really matters

Software is made by people, and in this game people are expensive, opinionated and slow to replace. A good studio is not the one with the most employees — it's the one where the right few people are working on the right things.

The five kinds of employee

RoleWhat they doWhen you need them
Designer Works the design document. Turns your feature list into something buildable. Always. Design is the phase that decides how good the product can be.
Lead Leads a design; also the person who makes a design better rather than just longer. Every serious project. A design with no lead runs at half speed and produces less.
Programmer Fills the code side of features, and fixes bugs in beta. In alpha and beta. One excellent programmer is worth several average ones in beta.
Artist Fills the art side of features. Depends entirely on the product. A 2D Editor needs lots of art; an antivirus needs almost none.
Service Accountants, receptionists, janitors, cooks, IT, security, support staff. As your office grows. They don't make products, they stop the studio from falling apart.

Skills and specialisations

Every employee has a skill level (shown as stars) and one or more specialisations — the areas they're unusually good at, such as 2D art, 3D, audio, or systems.

Features are also tagged with an area. A feature in 3D is built noticeably faster by someone with three stars in 3D than by a generalist — speed, not quality. This matters most for small teams: instead of hiring broadly, hire for the areas your chosen product actually needs.

Hiring checklist
  • Skill — the headline number. Higher is better, but it's not everything.
  • Specialisation — does it match the product you're about to build?
  • Traits — free bonuses or permanent annoyances. A fast learner becomes valuable; a permanently stressed employee is a liability in a small team.
  • Salary — a great employee at a painful salary can still be worth it. Three mediocre employees at the same total cost usually aren't.

Teams, and why bigger isn't better

You assign employees to teams, and teams to projects. The game gives you an ideal team size for each phase — roughly one person for every four months of work, plus one. Going much past that is one of the most common ways to waste money in Software Inc: each extra person adds less output than the last, while costing a full salary.

Don't solve a staffing problem by hiring more If a project is slow, the answer is almost always the wrong mix of roles, not "more people". Look at which bar is stuck first: that's the role you're short of.

Keeping people happy

Employees have needs — food, toilets, comfort, temperature, noise, cleanliness — and a mood that drifts up or down. Unhappy employees work slower, produce worse work and eventually resign. You don't need a palace; you need a workplace that isn't obviously annoying:

  • Enough desks, chairs and computers for everyone, and computers that aren't ancient.
  • Toilets and a kitchen/cafeteria within reasonable walking distance of the desks.
  • A comfortable temperature — offices get too hot with a crowd and too cold with none.
  • Somewhere to relax, and not too many people crammed into one room.
  • Enough service staff to keep the place clean and fed once you're past ~15 people.
Cheap morale wins Better chairs and a slightly bigger room are usually cheaper than a raise — and they help everyone at once. Keep an eye on the room's comfort rating rather than adding furniture at random.

Your first office

You don't need a nice office. You need a functional one, and the game will punish you for skipping the boring parts. A workable first build:

  1. One room for development, big enough for your team plus a bit of space. Cramming desks into a corridor makes everyone miserable.
  2. A desk, a chair and a computer for every person — and don't buy the cheapest computer if you're going to keep it for years.
  3. A bathroom and a small kitchen/cafeteria close by. If people have to walk across the building to eat, they spend the day walking.
  4. Heating and cooling. Rooms with lots of computers get hot; empty rooms get cold. This is one of the most commonly forgotten causes of a miserable studio.
  5. Reception and a little decoration once you can afford it — morale and first impressions, not a priority on day one.
Don't build the palace first A beautiful office with three employees in it is a slower, more expensive version of a small office with three employees in it. Expand when you hire, not before.

Training: the cheapest permanent buff

You can send employees on a one-month course from the education window. The course buys a star in a role + specialisation pair, at a cost that rises with the tier:

Course tierCostTimeWhat you get
First star$6001 monthA junior becomes useful in a specialisation.
Second star$2,0001 monthA generalist becomes a specialist.
Third star$5,0001 monthUnlocks the 15 % specialisation speed bonus and better bug fixing.
  • It's usually cheaper than hiring a new person at that skill — and the employee is already trained on your tools and your codebase.
  • They're unavailable while studying, so train between projects, not mid-alpha.
  • Skill also grows with experience; training is how you steer that growth toward the specialisations your products actually use.
  • Train your best programmer's bug-fixing skill before a big beta — it raises the ceiling on how many bugs you can fix.

HR: hiring on autopilot

Once you have more than one team, hiring becomes a full-time job. HR staff can take it over: you set a wage bracket, the roles and specialisations you want, whether to control role splits automatically, and they hire and fill the gaps for you.

  • Good for growth phases — you stop losing weeks to the hiring window.
  • Bad for tight budgets — an HR department that hires "what the teams need" can quietly overspend. Set the wage bracket conservatively.
  • HR doesn't replace judgement on key hires. Keep hiring your leads and star programmers yourself.

Lead designers and their demands

A lead designer is the difference between a design that's merely long and one that's actually good — and the good ones know it. Leads negotiate, and their demands are real:

04Money: staying alive long enough to get good

Most failed studios don't fail because their games are bad. They fail because they ran out of money between products. Treat cash flow as a first-class problem and the rest gets much easier.

Where money comes from

Product sales

Your main long-term income. A product sells strongly at launch, then tails off; updates and add-ons can revive that tail. Sequels sell to the audience you built.

Contracts

Other companies pay you to do work — support, bug fixing, small development jobs. Reliable, unglamorous income. Perfect early on, dangerous if it eats your best people permanently.

Publishing deals

A publisher funds and markets your product in exchange for a cut. Good when you're small and unknown; expensive once you can market yourself.

Platform cuts

If you own a distribution platform, every third-party sale through it pays you a percentage. A late-game income stream that compounds.

Where money goes

  • Salaries — every month, whether you shipped or not. This is the number that kills you.
  • Rent and utilities — grows with the office, quietly.
  • Marketing — optional, but the difference between a hit and a shrug.
  • Production — physical copies, hardware components, printing.
  • Licences and royalties — using someone else's tech or IP costs a share of sales.
  • Servers — products with online features cost money every month, forever.
Rule of thumb Keep at least three months of salaries in the bank before starting a big project. If you can't, take a contract first. Running out mid-project is the worst possible outcome — you lose the money and the time.

Loans: other people's money

When the bank balance is heading for zero, the loan window is the difference between a bad year and a dead company. You can borrow in steps of $10,000, choose a repayment period, and see the monthly cost before you sign.

LoanInterestMonthlyTotal repaid
$1,000,000 over 12 months~33 %~$111,000~$1.33 M
$1,000,000 over 24 months~42 %~$59,000~$1.42 M
$100,000 over 12 months~36 %~$11,300~$136,000
  • Interest is roughly 25 % plus a term that grows with the repayment period and shrinks the larger the loan is. Short and big is cheapest; long and small is most expensive.
  • You repay monthly, automatically — the payment keeps running while you develop, so borrow against a product you can finish, not a hope.
  • Repaying early is an accounting task, not a button. If you want the debt gone, staff your accountants.
  • Don't use loans to hire. Salaries are permanent; a loan is temporary. Borrow to bridge a development gap, not to raise your baseline costs.
What actually happens when you run out of money It isn't an instant game over. The game tries to cover the shortfall for you, in this order: bonds/insurance savings → investments → furniture inventory and awards → shares → subsidiary cash → a new loan. If all of that still doesn't cover the debt, the company fails. That's why investments and a stocked warehouse are a hidden safety net — and why awards are worth something after all.

Taxes: the bill you can shrink

Every so often the tax office wants its share of your profit. The rate depends on difficulty: 0 % on Beginner, 10 % Easy, 15 % Medium, 20 % Hard, 25 % Very Hard and Impossible.

Taxes are calculated from your income, operations, depreciation, investments and interest — and your accountants can legally grind the bill down by up to 5 % by working the tax report. That's a small percentage with a big absolute value once you're making millions.

The other way to shrink it There's an illegal route — "money funneling" — that moves money offshore and marks your tax report as dirty. It builds heat, and heat brings audits, confiscation and fines. See "The underworld" in the secrets chapter before you try it.

Insurance and safety

Two things in this game can destroy value overnight: a fire and a burglar. Insurance is how you make them survivable.

Contents insurance

  • Covers furniture that is stolen or destroyed in a fire.
  • The premium scales with what you own (roughly 2–8 % of inventory value per month).
  • You must pass a fire inspection first — no inspection, no insurance.

Health & life insurance

  • Health pays out if an employee becomes too sick to keep working.
  • Life pays out if an employee dies — most relevant for older staff.
  • Optional, but cheap cover against a sudden loss of a key employee.
  • Fire inspections are periodic and pass/fail. Fail and you pay a fee — and you can't insure your contents until you pass one.
  • Burglars steal items, not cash directly. Security staff stationed at entrances scare them off; sometimes the police catch them and recover some of the loot.
  • Keep fire safety in order: extinguishers/hoses and clear escape routes matter when the inspector visits.

The early-game survival plan

  1. Start small and cheap. One small product with a small team. Keep the office minimal.
  2. Take contracts to cover salaries while your first product is in development — but only as many as your spare people can handle.
  3. Reinvest in people, not furniture. Your first profits should buy one or two strong employees, not a bigger office.
  4. Build reputation before ambition. Three decent, finished products build a following that makes your first big product actually sell.
The death spiral Hire a big team for an ambitious product → run out of cash → take contracts with the same team → the product stalls and gets outdated → ship it late and unfinished → reputation drops → the next product sells worse. If you notice yourself in this loop, cut the team and finish something small.

Contracts: the early-game lifeline

Contracts are the least glamorous and most underrated part of the early game. Another company pays you a fixed amount to do a defined job. It doesn't build your reputation, and it doesn't build fans — but it pays salaries, which means it buys you time.

  • Take one when you're new. It's the difference between a comfortable first product and a panicked one.
  • Match the contract to spare people. If your designers are idle between designs, take a design-heavy contract. Don't pull your best programmer off the main project for months.
  • Watch the deadline. Late contracts damage your standing and can cost you the fee. If you can't finish it, don't take it.
  • Stop taking them once products pay the bills. Contracts have a ceiling; products don't.

05Selling: price, hype and the long tail

A finished product is only half the job. The other half is making sure people know it exists, charging the right amount, and then keeping it alive instead of letting it quietly die.

Marketing: spend it before launch

The game has several marketing tools, and they do different jobs:

ToolWhat it's forCostTiming
Press build Let journalists try an unfinished build. Builds followers steadily during development. Free Throughout alpha and beta
Press release A proper announcement push. The big spike of interest before launch. Money The last few months before release
Post-release marketing Keeps awareness up after launch. Helps the tail, doesn't create a second launch. Money, ongoing After release
Hype goes stale Marketing done years early is mostly wasted — hype fades on a deadline. Put your money into the last three to six months before release, not the first year of development.

Hype turns into followers, and followers turn into guaranteed sales. Think of your follower count as the number of people who will buy on day one almost regardless of reviews — they run out over the following months, which is why a big launch is worth so much.

Pricing: boring, and almost always right

The game tells you a suggested price for your product. It's derived from how much "value" the product actually contains — a small product gets a low suggested price, a big one gets a high one. That's the number to respect.

  • Price at or slightly below the suggestion. That's where the money is.
  • Don't price above it to "earn more". Buyers fall away fast, and past a certain point you sell nothing at all.
  • Don't discount early to "build an audience". Cutting the price permanently marks the product as cheap in the game's eyes — you don't get a bonus for it, you just avoid a penalty you could have avoided anyway.
  • If the product is small, price it small. An ambitious price on a thin product reads as overpriced, and reviewers will say so.
Why pricing feels unfair The suggested price is tied to how much of the type's "expected content" you actually built. A product with half the expected features is compared against half the expected price. Charging full price for it counts as massively overpriced — even if it took you just as long to make.

Subscriptions: recurring money, honestly explained

When you design a product there's a subscription toggle. It replaces the one-off price with a monthly fee, and it changes the whole shape of the product's income.

One-off price

  • You get paid once per customer, at launch.
  • Income is a spike followed by a long, fading tail.
  • Every copy sold is money in the bank immediately.
  • Works for anything, including physical boxes.

Subscription

  • You get paid every month by everyone still subscribed.
  • Income is small at first and grows into a stock of paying users.
  • Revenue continues as long as people stay — and stops when they leave.
  • Needs a product people keep using.

The game sets the suggested subscription price at roughly 8 % of the one-off price. An $80 game becomes about $6.40 a month, so a customer has to stay for about a year and a month just to pay what they would have paid once.

The rule of thumb Subscriptions are a bet on retention. A category's retention is how fast customers leave: operating systems keep people for years, sports games lose most of their audience within a year. In a long-retention category a subscriber is worth several one-off sales; in a short-retention one they're worth a fraction of one.
CategoryRetentionDoes subscription make sense?
Operating systems (computer / console / phone)36–72 monthsYes — the only category where it clearly wins. Customers stay for years.
Editors, audio tools, RPG/RTS games24 monthsMarginal. You'll earn less than a one-off price, but you get steady income.
Adventure / simulation games18 monthsNo — too much churn for an 8 % monthly price.
Antivirus16 monthsNo. It churns faster than you'd expect.
Office software, sports, FPS12 monthsNo. These are built for a new full-price release every year.

Things the toggle doesn't tell you:

  • You can't put hardware on a subscription. The toggle is hidden for consoles and phones.
  • Physical copies of a subscription are a trap. Retail takes a far bigger cut on them, to the point where boxed subscriptions usually lose money — treat subscriptions as digital-only.
  • Converting an existing series is penalised. A sequel that switches an old one-off franchise to subscriptions loses part of its sequel bonus, and reviewers comment on the switch. Starting a new IP as a subscription is cleaner.
  • Paid add-ons sell worse to subscribers. The game halves their appeal to a subscription audience, so plan add-ons as a perk rather than a second income stream.
  • Subscribers count as your user base — which matters enormously for an operating system, where the size of your user base decides how much third-party software can sell.
If you try it Price at the suggestion, launch with a genuinely finished product (a subscriber who leaves in month one is worth almost nothing), and commit to a steady stream of updates. A subscription product without updates is just a worse one-off product.

Release timing

Sales have a seasonal curve. November and December are the strongest months; July and August are the weakest. The difference is large — around a third more demand in December than in August. If a product is finished in early summer, it is often worth holding it back a couple of months, especially if it's a big release.

The long tail: updates, add-ons, sequels

Updates

Free patches. They fix bugs, modernise the tech, and reset part of the product's decline — sales pick up again. The cheapest way to keep money coming in from something you already built.

Add-ons & expansions

Sold to the people who already own the base product, and they boost the base product's sales too. Great value: you already have the audience, the engine and the assets.

Sequels

Sell to your existing fans and inherit some of the previous game's reputation. But a sequel that's worse than the original damages the whole series — never rush one.

The tail rule Every product declines over time, and the decline is slowest in "serious" categories (operating systems, tools) and fastest in fashion-driven ones (sports games, shooters). A product with a long decline is worth more updates; a product with a short one is better replaced by a sequel.

Ports: sell to a second platform

If a product only supports one operating system, you're only selling to that system's users. Porting brings a released product to another OS: it's a short project (a couple of months), and afterwards the product counts as supported on that platform — reaching its entire user base.

  • Port late, not early. The port sells to people who couldn't buy before; the bigger the other platform's user base, the more it's worth.
  • Port your long-tail products first. A game with 24-month retention earns from a port for years; a sports game with 12 months earns briefly.
  • Ports are cheap compared with a new product — same code, new audience.
When to skip it If your product already supports every major OS, or the other platform is tiny, a port is busywork. Check the platform's user base before committing the team.

Publishers: money now, revenue later

A publisher offers you a deal: they put up funding and/or handle marketing or printing, and in exchange they take a royalty on sales — up to 60 % — usually with a recoup clause (they keep the first revenue until they've recovered what they spent) and a deadline.

Take the deal when…

  • You can't afford marketing for a product you believe in.
  • You're unknown and need distribution more than margin.
  • The funding lets you finish a product you'd otherwise abandon.

Avoid it when…

  • You can market the product yourself — the royalty is permanent.
  • You're already famous in that category; you're giving away money.
  • The deadline is tight and the penalty is harsh.

Support: the bill for shipping bugs

Every released product generates support tickets — the more users and the more bugs, the faster they arrive. If nobody answers them, they go stale and you lose fans in that category, and the loss per missed ticket is bigger the buggier the product was.

  • Staff a support team once you have a catalogue, or your back catalogue quietly erodes the reputation your next launch depends on.
  • Fewer bugs = fewer tickets. Support cost is another reason to fix the build before release.
  • Support work can be sold — taking support deals for other companies' products is a steady, unglamorous income stream.
  • Your store has its own penalty if it's the product being neglected — distribution platforms lose standing rather than fans.

Where you sell

In the early years almost everything is sold in boxes: you print copies, shops take a cut, and you can run out of stock. As the years pass, digital distribution takes over — first through other people's platforms (which take a percentage), then, if you build one, through your own.

  • Early game: keep physical copies in stock before launch. A sell-out is lost money.
  • Mid game: get onto platforms. They take a cut, but they reach far more people.
  • Late game: owning a distribution platform means everyone else's sales pay you a percentage. It's one of the strongest long-term investments in the game.

06Servers and hardware

Two systems the tutorial barely explains, and that you can happily ignore for your first few years. The moment a product needs online features — or you decide to build a console — you need to understand them, because both can eat a lot of money quietly.

Servers: when your software needs the internet

Some features are online by nature. You'll see the game warn you in the design window when you pick them — that's your cue to think about hosting:

ProductFeatures that need servers
GamesMMO
Distribution platformsCloud sync, social features, voice chat, remote streaming
AntivirusVirus definitions, data mining
Operating systemsAuto-update, app store, cloud backup, microphone surveillance
Office softwareCloud backup

If you release a product with those features and no hosting arranged, customers get very angry — the game tells you this in plain text at design time. There are two ways to provide it:

Host it yourself

  • Buy server equipment (small servers up to big server racks) and place it in a room.
  • Connect servers together into a group — load is shared across the group.
  • Assign the product to that group.
  • Cheapest long-term; you pay for the hardware, the electricity and the cooling.
  • If a server in the group breaks, the whole group stops working.

Pay someone else

  • Take a hosting deal: another company runs the servers for you.
  • You pay a monthly fee instead of buying hardware.
  • Good when your product is huge and you don't want to build a server room.
  • You lose a bit of control — and the fee never stops while the deal runs.
Load is not a one-off cost A product's server load is its requirement multiplied by how many people own it. The more successful the product, the more capacity it eats. A hit online game can force you to keep buying servers for years — plan the cost, not just the launch.

When a server group can't keep up, it doesn't just "lag". You lose users, your awareness drops, you lose fans, and refunds go up. The game will warn you that servers are having trouble dealing with their load — treat that as an emergency, not a nuisance.

The best early use of a server: SCM

Before you need servers for a product, put source control management on one. It makes every project in the studio generate fewer bugs and run a little faster. It is the cheapest, most boring, most consistently valuable purchase in the building.

Other things servers are for
  • Your own digital distribution platform needs a server — and owning a store is one of the strongest late-game income streams.
  • IT staff repair servers and computers. Without them, broken equipment stays broken.
  • Cooling. A room full of servers gets very hot, very fast. Deal with temperature or your employees will suffer for it.
"You can develop your own server products…" — where that line comes from

That sentence is from the overhaul announcement on Steam (the "Overhaul update" post, 5 Aug 2026), not from a patch you have installed. The overhaul is the big pre-1.0 rewrite of marketing, servers and subsidiaries; as of the latest news it is still heading into testing, and the developers have said it breaks save compatibility.

In the version this guide covers (1.8.42) servers are equipment you buy, place and wire together, and hosting is either in-house or outsourced through a deal — the rules above are exactly what applies. The overhaul turns that into a product line:

  • Server products become something you design, with features that set their bandwidth, durability, power usage and security.
  • In-world servers must be populated with those products — bought from the market or manufactured yourself. The server furniture becomes the chassis; the product is the machine inside.
  • Security lowers the impact of DDoS attacks, and IT staff work to mitigate them.
  • DDoS becomes a random event that scales with your company's size (and can be aimed at other players in multiplayer) instead of a $5M action you buy.

If your build is on an overhaul test branch, expect these rules to apply on top of everything above: hosting still costs bandwidth, it just comes from a product you chose rather than a rack you bought.

DDoS today: a $5 million grudge

In the current build there is exactly one way a DDoS happens, and it's the one you cause:

  • You open the accounting/actions window and pick DDoS — it costs $5,000,000.
  • You then choose a rival product that needs servers and isn't hosted by a third party.
  • The attack runs for 12 months. Every day it applies load incidents to that product: their user base shrinks, their awareness drops, they lose fans, and their refunds rise.
  • It is one-way in single player: AI companies never DDoS you, and you can't target another player in multiplayer.

The overhaul changes this into a two-way, random system with a defence stat — see the note above.

Hardware: consoles and phones

Hardware is the other business in Software Inc. Instead of writing software for someone else's machine, you build the machine — and then you own the platform everyone else has to sell on.

  • Where it lives: the Console and Phone categories of the Operating System type. Their suggested prices are far higher than software (consoles around 400, phones around 500), and they keep their audience for years.
  • Design: in the hardware editor you shape the device and choose its components — plastic, antenna, motherboard, drive bay, controller, case, screen, battery and so on. Each component has a price and a build time, so a fancier device costs more to make and takes longer to produce.
  • Manufacturing: an assembly line turns components into finished units. Hardware is 100 % physical — there is no digital version. Unsold stock is money you have already spent.
  • Features that need components are worth more on hardware than on software, so a good device design genuinely improves the product rather than just decorating it.
  • Peripherals: a hardware product can be tied to a peripheral add-on, like a console that ships with a joystick. The peripheral's quality multiplies the main product's quality, so a bad one drags the whole device down.
Hardware is not a beginner move Margins are thinner, and every unit you don't sell is a component you already paid for. A known brand also gets more out of hardware than an unknown one, so the reputation you build for years is what makes a device launch work. Do it when you have cash you can afford to lose — and when you want a platform, not just a product.

The economics of a device

A hardware unit is not a software copy. Every box costs real money to make, and the number that governs everything is the bill of materials — the sum of the components your design requires.

Unit economics, roughly:
sale price − retail cut (~30 %) − cost of the unit = profit per box
Cost of the unit ≈ the component bill if you make it yourself; about twice that if you simply buy finished units.

Components come from the features you ticked. Anything marked with a requirement only appears if the matching feature is in the design, so every feature carries a hidden per-unit price tag. The shipped numbers make this concrete:

The hardware trap Ticking a feature that requires a $50 touch sensor adds about $50 of material to every unit you will ever build. On software, a feature costs development time once. Make, don't buy You can shortcut production by ordering finished copies, but you pay roughly double the component bill for the privilege — and a phone can go from profitable to loss-making on that markup alone. An assembly line pays for itself quickly on anything you intend to sell in volume.

Hardware checklist

  1. Keep the bill of materials lean. Decide which component-heavy features actually pay for themselves. Expensive parts in the shipped data: controller chip ($100 on phones), touch sensor ($50), screen ($40). Cheap: plastic, case, motherboard ($5–$10).
  2. Build the factory before you order. An assembly line needs a printer for every component plus one for each assembly step, including the final assembly. Throughput is set by the slowest station, so one under-specced printer bottlenecks the whole line.
  3. Produce stock before launch. Hardware is 100 % physical. If you launch with no stock, every sale you could have made is missed, and missed demand does not come back later.
  4. But don't over-order. Unsold units are components you already paid for. Estimate the first few months of sales, then top up as you see the real curve.
  5. Check the price against the bill, not just the suggestion. The suggested price is an anchor, not a promise. If the numbers don't work, either cut components or accept that the device is a loss-leader for the platform.
  6. Think in years, not weeks. Console users stick around for a long time (retention 48 months), so a console can be a platform play: every piece of software sold for your OS depends on your user base. That is the real prize, not the box margin.
The hardware design itself The hardware editor is where you shape the device (its body, controls, screen placement). It is mostly about how the product looks — if you skip it, the game warns you and generates a random design. Do it anyway: it's the difference between "my console" and "a grey box".

07Reviews and reputation: what actually matters

Reviews are the most misunderstood part of Software Inc. There are two completely different systems with the same name, and only one of them is a game mechanic.

Reviews during development

  • You pay for them and they take a little time.
  • They tell you how good the product currently is, per area.
  • They unlock an iteration: a second design pass that raises the quality ceiling.
  • They also reduce bugs slightly at the start of beta.
  • Genuinely useful. Run one before beta if you can afford it.

Press reviews after release

  • Free, automatic, and they show up in the newspaper.
  • Several outlets score you out of five stars with a quote.
  • They're fun, they're good feedback, and they don't change sales at all.
  • What they reflect — quality, hype, value — is what already decided your sales.

So: chase the things that cause good reviews (finish the product, design properly, fix bugs, price fairly), not the reviews themselves. A one-star press review on a well-made product doesn't cost you a penny in sales; it just stings.

Reputation is the real long game

Your company has a reputation in each product category, backed by a pool of fans. It builds slowly and matters enormously:

  • Unknown studios sell less. Early on, even a good product undersells because nobody knows who you are. This is normal — don't panic and don't overprice to compensate.
  • Good products build fans. Fans make the next launch bigger and make marketing go further.
  • Bad products lose fans. Shipping something cheap and broken doesn't just fail to help — it actively sets you back.
  • Reputation is per category. Being beloved for games doesn't help you sell antivirus. If you switch categories, expect to start over.
The compounding trick Pick a category you like and stay in it. Product, update, expansion, sequel — each one builds the same audience and the same reputation. A studio that jumps between categories every release never gets the compounding effect, and always feels like it's starting over.

Awards, and the other reputation

There are two reputations in this game, and only one of them is about your products.

Awards (prestige)

  • Every year the industry hands out awards by product category — gold, silver and bronze, plus a platinum trophy for five wins in the same category.
  • You get a trophy you can place in the office. In 1.8.42 they're prestige only — no direct sales or reputation bonus.
  • They do have a cash value: if you go bankrupt, the game will sell your awards to cover the debt.

Business reputation

  • A separate six-star track, starting at about 1/6.
  • It isn't about games — it's about whether companies trust you.
  • Gates hiring (candidates with high demands won't join an unknown company) and deals (the chance a company offers you one).
  • Goes up when you complete contracts and deals, down when you fail them — and tax fraud costs a large chunk of it.
How to raise business reputation Take contracts and finish them on time. It's slow, unglamorous and the single best way to get access to better hires and bigger deals. Failing a deal is expensive in ways the tooltip doesn't show — it lowers the ceiling on everything you can sign next.

What to do about a bad release

  1. Patch it. Updates fix bugs and refresh sales — and are much cheaper than a new game.
  2. Don't immediately sequel it. A sequel to a badly received product inherits the damage.
  3. Rebuild in a small, safe product. A modest, finished, well-priced release in the same category repairs reputation faster than a risky big swing.

08Technology: don't ship yesterday's product

Every area of software improves over the years. A product built on old technology is still a product — but it will be reviewed as outdated and it will sell worse than the same product built on current technology.

  • Research continuously. It's a small ongoing cost compared with the penalty for falling behind. Treat it like rent, not like a project.
  • Licence technology when you must. If you're behind in an area, you can use someone else's tech — for a fee or a royalty on sales. Cheaper than missing the market; more expensive than having researched it yourself.
  • Match tech to the product. Building a modern product on an old engine gives you a product that looks old on day one, and reviewers will say exactly that.
  • Train your people. Employees improve with experience, and formal training speeds that up. A studio that never trains ends up with expensive people who have stopped growing.
How to spot it If a product's reviews mention that it feels dated — or if the product screen warns that the tech is behind — that's the signal. It's much cheaper to fix before release than after.

09Growing: from one product to an empire

Once you can reliably ship a profitable product, the game opens up. These are the directions you can grow in — roughly in order of how much they cost and how much they change the game.

1. Run more than one project

The obvious next step: two products at once, with two separate teams. The trap is splitting one team across two projects — everyone works slowly on both. Build a second team, don't divide the first one.

2. Build a series

Sequels, expansions and updates to the same product line are the most reliable money in the game. You already have the audience, the technology and usually the assets. A well-supported series can fund everything else you want to do.

3. Own the store

Building a distribution platform is expensive and slow, and early on it looks like a waste. Then it starts taking a cut of everyone's sales, including your competitors', and it becomes the most valuable thing you own.

4. Make hardware

Consoles and phones are a different business: you design the device, buy components, manufacture physical units and sell boxes. Margins are thinner and mistakes are more expensive — a hardware product that sells poorly still cost you the components. Do it when you have cash to burn.

5. Publish other studios

If you have money and reputation, you can fund and publish other companies' products for a share of the revenue. Lower risk than making your own product, lower ceiling too.

6. Buy things

Stocks, subsidiaries and takeovers are the late-game layer. They're a way to convert a large cash pile into ongoing income and control — and a good way to lose a large cash pile if you're careless.

WhatWhat you getRisk
Shares Dividends, a rising share price, and voting power in another company. The price can fall; your money is locked in until you sell.
Majority stake Control of the company — you decide what it develops. You inherit its debts and its problems.
Subsidiaries A company that keeps developing products on its own while you own it. They can lose money; you may have to bail them out.
Bonds & savings A safe place for idle cash, earning a little interest. Low return — but it's the first thing the game liquidates if you go bankrupt.
The boring truth about the late game Buying shares in a well-run competitor is usually a better return than buying a badly-run one cheaply. And cash sitting in bonds is not wasted — it's the buffer that saves you from a bad launch, and the bankruptcy system will spend it before it spends anything else.
Order of operations Profitable single product → a second team → a series → a platform or hardware. Every time you skip a step, you're gambling the studio on a mechanic you haven't practised yet.

10Secrets and things the game doesn't tell you

A grab bag of quirks, hidden effects and small exploits of the rules. None of them are cheating — they're just the parts of the simulation that never make it into a tooltip.

Rules quirks

  1. The press review doesn't sell games. The five-star newspaper review is cosmetic. Your sales come from quality, hype, price and reputation. Stop chasing stars; chase the things that cause them.
  2. The fourth design pass removes luck. Three passes reach the maximum quality ceiling but leave a wide random roll. A fourth pass tightens that roll to almost nothing — you get what you planned, every time.
  3. A review iteration can beat your design ceiling. It's the only way to raise a product's quality ceiling after design. It costs up to half your progress, and each iteration needs its own finished review first — but you can do it more than once before beta, and on a small project the ceiling gain is large.
  4. Discounts never pay. Price history is a ratchet: once a product has been cheaper, it carries a permanent penalty at any higher price. Cutting the price doesn't create demand — it only avoids a penalty you could have avoided by pricing right the first time.
  5. Hype has a shelf life of about three months. Marketing earlier than that mostly fades before launch. Spend it late.
  6. Followers are a floor on sales, not a multiplier. Around a tenth of them convert into guaranteed units every month. Even a badly reviewed product sells to its followers — which is why pre-release hype is worth so much.
  7. Your own back catalogue competes with you. Quality is judged as a share of everything selling in the category — including your older products still on shelves. Flooding a category with slightly-improved re-releases hurts your own new release.
  8. Fans leak between categories. A strong category spills part of its audience into sibling categories, and a little across entirely different software types. That's why adjacent products help each other more than unrelated ones.
  9. Old products stop eating servers. After roughly six months, products with tiny server needs drop off hosting automatically. You don't have to maintain your whole back catalogue forever.
  10. Server overload costs fans, not just users. Running out of capacity reduces awareness and takes fans away — a double hit on your next launch.

Money quirks

  • Lead designers can own the IP. If your lead owns it, part of every sale goes to them — forever. "Reverse Uno" is the game's way of avoiding that, and it's why a designer who demands IP ownership is a much bigger deal than their salary.
  • Physical sales have hidden costs. Shops take roughly 30 % of the retail price, printing costs about $2 per copy (double for hardware), a box holds 1,000 copies, and a sell-out is lost demand — it does not move to digital.
  • Hardware is always physical. There's no digital escape hatch for a console: every unit is a component you paid for before you knew whether it would sell.
  • Peripherals count. A forced add-on like a joystick multiplies the main device's quality — a cheap peripheral can sink an otherwise good console.
  • You can sabotage rivals. Deep into the game there's a very expensive action that DDoSes a competitor's online product: their refunds spike and their reputation suffers. It works both ways, so protect your own online products.

People quirks

  • Traits matter more than they look. A fast learner becomes a senior employee in a fraction of the time; a "born leader" makes everyone around them better; a permanently stressed employee is a liability in a small team. Read the trait list before you hire.
  • Specialisations are a speed bonus, not a quality bonus. A three-star specialisation in the area a feature belongs to makes that work meaningfully faster. Quality still comes from raw skill — so a specialist is faster, not automatically better.
  • Your best programmer caps beta, and nothing else does. Not team size, not time, not money. One strong programmer is a strategic asset.
  • Source control on a server improves every project — fewer bugs, slightly faster work. It's the cheapest permanent buff in the game.

11How to win: two playbooks

Everything so far is mechanics. This is the strategy layer: what to build, in what order, with the money you actually have. Software and hardware are different games with different rhythms — treat them as two playbooks, not one.

The one principle behind every good studio

Compounding beats brilliance. One good product in a category you keep returning to is worth more than five scattered hits, because reputation, fans, technology and reusable features all carry forward.

Almost every mechanic in the game rewards this: fans accumulate per category, sequels inherit reputation, features get cheaper to reuse, updates extend the tail, and your existing audience makes the next launch bigger. The studio that jumps categories every release is permanently starting from zero.

The product loop, as a habit

  1. Design three or four passes. Choose ★★ features, balance the three areas, put a lead on it.
  2. Alpha with the right team. Match roles to the feature list; don't bloat the team.
  3. Review, then iterate if it's worth it. On a small project an iteration is usually worth the progress it costs.
  4. Beta with your best programmer. Ship when the fix curve flattens, not at zero bugs.
  5. Market in the last three months. Press builds throughout, a press release near the end.
  6. Release into autumn if you can. Price at the suggestion.
  7. Patch at about half the category's retention. That's when the sales tail is dying.
  8. Expansion at around a year, sequel at around two. That cadence keeps the audience warm and the cash coming.
The pre-release checklist Design passes done · every feature finished · best programmer on beta · price at the suggestion · marketing booked for the last three months · stock printed (physical) · servers assigned (online features) · release month chosen. If any of those is missing, the launch will underperform — and you'll usually know which one it was.

The hardware playbook

Hardware is a different game: slower, more expensive, and far more strategic. Don't start one because you're bored of software — start one because you want the platform.

Before you start

  • Cash you can lose. A device costs components up front, and unsold units are dead money.
  • A reputation worth putting on a box. The quality of a known brand's device is judged higher.
  • A reason. A console or phone is worth more as a platform for your software than as a product.
  • Factory space. Assembly lines need room, power and cooling.

While you build it

  • Design lean. Every component-heavy feature is a per-unit cost forever.
  • Build the line first. A printer for every component plus the final assembly; the slowest station sets the rate.
  • Stock before launch. Launching with no boxes is throwing away demand.
  • Price against the bill of materials, not against the suggested price alone.
The platform dividend A successful console or phone gives you a user base that stays for years, and every piece of software sold for that operating system depends on it. That's the real hardware business: the box is the entry ticket, the software sales are the prize.

The subscription playbook

  • Only in long-retention categories. Operating systems, above all: 36–72 months of retention is what makes an 8 % monthly price beat a one-off sale. Tools at 24 months are marginal; games, antivirus and office software churn too fast to make it work.
  • Start a new IP as a subscription rather than converting an established one-off series; conversion loses part of the sequel bonus and gets called out in reviews.
  • Price at the suggestion and keep the product updated — churn is the enemy, and updates are the only weapon against it.
  • Keep it digital. Boxed subscriptions are punished by the retail cut.
  • Think in years. A subscription product earns little at launch and a lot later; if you need cash now, it's the wrong model.

When a year goes badly

  1. Cut costs first. Reduce the team to what the next product actually needs; a smaller team finishing something beats a big team stalling.
  2. Finish something small and good. Reputation is repaired by finished, fairly-priced products, not by ambition.
  3. Update the back catalogue. Old products still have an audience, and updates are the cheapest revenue in the game.
  4. Take a contract. It doesn't build reputation, but it buys the months you need to rebuild.
  5. Don't swing for the fences. The comeback product should be the safest thing you can make well, not the most ambitious thing you can imagine.

4Hiring ahead of revenue Salaries keep running while the product is late; you take contracts to survive and the product gets later. Three months of salaries in the bank before any big project.
5Overstaffing a project Each extra person adds less than they cost, and the office gets worse for everyone. Use the game's suggested team size per phase.
6Overpricing Sales fall off a cliff; past a certain multiple of the suggested price, nobody buys at all. Price at or just below the suggestion.
7Marketing too early The hype has faded by launch; you paid for attention nobody remembers. Concentrate marketing in the last few months.
8Releasing in summer You launch into the weakest months of the year for no reason. Hold for autumn/winter if the product is ready early.
9Abandoning products The sales tail dies, and the audience you built forgets you. Patch, update, expand. The tail is where the profit is.
10Category hopping Every new category resets your reputation; every launch feels like your first. Pick a category and build a series before expanding.

Small glossary

TermPlain meaning
Design documentWhere you choose the product's features. Repeated passes raise the quality ceiling.
AlphaThe long build phase. Code and art bars fill; bugs accumulate.
BetaBug-fixing phase before release.
IterationA second design pass after a review. Raises the quality ceiling; costs progress.
Lead designerThe person who makes a design better rather than just longer. Without one, design is slow and weak.
Optimal dev timeThe length the game thinks suits this type of product. Going far past it rarely pays.
Hype / followersPre-release interest. Followers become guaranteed sales at launch.
FansYour permanent audience in a category. They make every future launch bigger.
ReputationHow well known and trusted you are in a category. Gates your sales when you're new.
Tech levelHow modern the underlying technology is. Old tech means "outdated" reviews.
Add-onExtra content sold on top of a released product; also boosts the base product's sales.
ContractPaid work for another company. Reliable income, but it occupies your staff.
PublisherFunds and markets your product for a share of the revenue.
Distribution platformA store. Others take a cut of your sales; owning one pays you a cut of theirs.
ServerThe hardware that runs your online features. Buy it, place it, wire it into a group, assign products to it.
Bandwidth / loadHow much server capacity your products consume. Load = requirement × number of users.
HostingWho runs the servers for a product: you (in-house) or another company (a hosting deal).
SCMSource control management. Put it on a server for fewer bugs and faster development across the studio.
HardwarePhysical devices (consoles, phones). You design the device, manufacture units and sell boxes.
ManufacturingTurning components into sellable hardware units. Unsold units are money already spent.
PeripheralA forced add-on sold with a hardware product (e.g. a joystick). Its quality affects the device's quality.
Bill of materialsThe sum of the components your device design needs. It is the per-unit cost you pay before retail's cut.
Assembly lineThe set of printers that turns components into finished units. The slowest station sets the rate.
SubscriptionA monthly fee instead of a one-off price. Only worth it in categories where customers stay for a long time.
RetentionHow fast a product's audience leaves. Long retention = a long sales tail and a viable subscription.
PlatformOwning the operating system or store other software has to go through — the real prize of hardware.
PortBringing a product to another operating system to reach more buyers.
If you're stuck Ask which phase you're in, then check the one thing that phase depends on: Design → did you run enough passes? Alpha → is the missing role the bottleneck? Beta → is your best programmer good enough? Release → is the price near the suggestion, and did you market in the last few months? Nine times out of ten, the answer is there.

And if a product is selling but the numbers keep getting worse, check its servers: running out of bandwidth costs you users, fans and reputation at the same time.
