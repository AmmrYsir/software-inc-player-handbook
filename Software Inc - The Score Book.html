    00What "score" actually means in this game

    Software Inc never shows you a single "score". It shows you at least five different numbers that all look like scores, and players routinely confuse them. Before any formula makes sense, you need to know which one you are looking at.

    NumberWhere you see itRangeWhat it really is
    RealQuality Product detail window, review text 0 – 1 The build quality produced by your dev team. Fixed at release.
    Quality[3] Never directly; drives everything else 0 – 1 × 3 Per-submarket quality (e.g. Gameplay / Graphics / Story). Your features are scored into these three buckets.
    Market quality (Qmw) Never shown directly; it is the sales driver 0 – 1 Your quality as a share of all quality in the category, weighted by your submarket mix.
    Review stars Review window, release news 1 – 5 ★ Seven fictional outlets each score you; the game shows the three with the strongest opinions.
    Positive review % Product window after release 0 – 100 % Cosmetic. Generated from the product's target score and sales volume, and consumed only by the UI.
    The one-sentence version Your features are converted into spec points; dev work converts spec points into real quality; real quality plus hype, price and reputation becomes market quality; market quality plus awareness becomes sales. Every "score" in the UI is a view of one of those stages.

    The pipeline, end to end

    Stage 1 · Design

    Spec points

    Each feature you pick contributes DevTime × level impact points, split across the 3 submarkets. This defines the maximum quality you can reach.

    Stage 2 · Alpha

    Real quality

    Programmers and artists turn spec points into code/art quality. Team size, skill stars and time decide how close you get to the ceiling.

    Stage 3 · Beta

    Bugs & polish

    Bugs accumulate while working. Bug count becomes a multiplier (BugScore) that can erase a large part of your quality in reviews.

    Stage 4 · Market

    Stars & sales

    Reviews, hype, price and reputation produce the daily sales curve, which decays with the category's Retention.

    01Spec points: how features become score

    Everything starts in the design document. When you tick a feature, the game records its DevTime and its Level. Those two numbers are the raw "points" of the whole game — they determine both how long development takes and how much quality the finished product can possibly have.

    The level impact table

    Each feature has a level from 1 to 3 (shown as stars in the UI). The game multiplies the feature's dev time by a fixed table to get spec points:

    Spec points contributed by one featurespecDevTime = DevTime × SpecLevelImpact[Level] SpecLevelImpact = { 0: 0.5, 1: 1.5, 2: 3.0, 3: 0.0 }

    FeatureBase.cs:13 SpecLevelImpact; FeatureBase.cs:42 SpecDevTime

    LevelUI labelMultiplierMeaning
    0Spec feature0.5×The category-defining feature (e.g. "Game design", "2D graphics"). Cheap in points.
    1★1.5×Common feature. 1 dev month = 1.5 spec points.
    2★★3.0×Premium feature. Double the point efficiency of a ★ feature.
    3★★★0.0×Script feature. Gives zero spec points; its value is a runtime script (e.g. bit mining).
    Why ★★ features are the core of optimisation A ★★ feature converts dev time into quality at twice the rate of a ★ feature and is ignored for nothing else. When two features cost the same time, the higher level one is almost always correct — unless you specifically need its submarket distribution.

    Where the points go: three submarkets

    Every software type defines exactly three submarkets (for games: Gameplay, Graphics, Story). Every feature carries a 3-value Submarkets vector, and the points are split along it. Points are then normalised by the type's optimal dev time and compared against the category's submarket equilibrium.

    Points a feature adds to each submarket (FeatureBase.GetSubAdd)points[j] = Submarkets[j] × GetSubScale(tech, category, quality) × specDevTime / SoftwareType.OptimalDevTime // hardware categories only (console/phone): specDevTime is multiplied by // HardwareFactor (default 6) when the component depends on the feature

    FeatureBase.cs:263–323 GetSubAdd, GetHardwareScale

    How points are judged — per spec, per submarket (SoftwareCategory.GetFinalScoreFromSummary)for each spec s, for each submarket j: share[s][j] = min(1, summation[s][j] × SubScale(tech[s], cat, quality[j]) / Submarkets[j]) out[j] += share[s][j] × Submarkets[j] // and, separately, the design's total "perceived value": perceivedValue = min(1, Σfeatures specDevTime × hwScale / OptimalDevTime)

    SoftwareCategory.cs:367–383; SoftwareCategory.cs:321–334 PerceivedValue; FeatureBase.cs:263–323

    Two different ceilings — do not mix them up 1. Per-spec saturation. For each spec (the category-defining feature such as "Game design" or "2D graphics"), the contribution to a submarket is capped at that submarket's target. One spec cannot carry a submarket on its own, but two specs can each contribute a full target, so the product total is not capped. This is why a broad design (many specs) out-scores a lopsided one.
    2. Total design mass saturation. PerceivedValue stops at 1.0 once the sum of feature spec-time reaches the type's OptimalDevTime. Every extra month of features beyond that adds no perceived value — it only raises dev cost and bug count. This is the real "wasted points" wall, and it is what SubmarketScoreWasted measures.

    Note the two different reference vectors. SubmarketEq is the category's ideal mix, normalised to sum to 1; it is what the automatic design scorer compares candidate feature sets against. The product's own Submarkets vector is the cap used by GetFinalScoreFromSummary and the weight used for market-weighted quality. Both matter, and they are read from different places.

    The practical consequence: level does not change dev time, only point yield. A ★★ feature and a ★ feature with equal DevTime cost the same months, but the ★★ one contributes twice the spec points. The efficient pick is almost always the highest-level feature per month.

    02Real quality: turning months into a number

    RealQuality is not an average of your features — it is the product of two things: how complete each feature is (progress) and how well it was made (quality), combined by a formula that punishes unfinished work far more than mediocre work.

    The design document sets your quality ceiling

    This is the single most important fact in the whole guide. When a project leaves the design phase, every feature gets a hidden target quality rolled from a Gaussian whose mean is decided by the number of design passes you did:

    FeatureProgress.Reset — target quality after N design passesprogress = passes_completed // 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 CodeTargetQual = ArtTargetQual = GaussClamp( Means[progress], Deviations[progress] ) Means = { 0, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.0 } Deviations = { 0.10, 0.20, 0.10, 0.10, 0.01 }

    SoftwareWorkItem.cs:276–283 (Reset), 59–61 (Means/Deviations)

    Design passesTarget qualitySpreadWhat it means
    00.00± 0.10Straight to alpha with no design work — the project is born broken.
    10.50± 0.20Half quality ceiling, huge variance. Cheap but very risky.
    20.75± 0.10The default "good enough" pass count.
    31.00± 0.10Full ceiling with normal variance.
    41.00± 0.01Full ceiling, almost deterministic. The 4th pass removes variance.

    Passes are counted by Iteration; the design target is (Iteration + 1) × DevTime, so each pass costs one full project's worth of feature time, and the loop stops after Iteration 3 (four passes). Design work accrues at PerHour(1 − DesignRatio) = 2/3 of a month per hour — twice the alpha rate (1/3) — then multiplied by the difficulty's DesignDocumentSpeedBonus (3× / 3× / 2× / 2× / 1× / 1×). Without a lead designer on the project, design runs at 50 % speed.

    Practical rule Never ship a project with fewer than 3 design passes unless you are deliberately making a cheap cash-grab. The 4th pass exists to kill variance: 3 passes can roll 0.90, 4 passes essentially always roll 0.99–1.00.

    Alpha: how quality actually accumulates

    FeatureProgress.AddQuality — per work tickQual += weight × 1.5 × (0.75 + 0.25 × skill) × CodeTargetQual / CDevTime // clamped to CodeTargetQual (same for Qual2 / art)

    SoftwareWorkItem.cs:261–274

    Read that carefully: the accumulating quality is 0.75 + 0.25 × skill. A 5-star programmer contributes at most 1.00; a 0-star programmer still contributes 0.75. Skill matters, but the design ceiling matters four times more. When a feature is fully worked, quality clamps at its target — extra work cannot exceed the design ceiling.

    Speed damping — the same feature slows down as it approaches completionspeed = _speedDamper.Evaluate( Progress / (DevTime × 4) ) _speedDamper(x) = −1.35x² + 1.45 // 1.45 at the start → 0.10 at completion

    SoftwareWorkItem.cs:57; FeatureProgress.cs region 175–259

    Code vs art: the CodeArtRatio blend

    Every feature has a CodeArt value between 0 and 1 — the share of its work that is programming. The product's overall ratio is dev-time weighted:

    SoftwareType.CodeArtRatioratio = Σ (feature.CodeArt × feature.DevTime) / Σ feature.DevTime // per-feature ratio used inside FinalQualityCalc: ratiof = CDevTime / (CDevTime + ADevTime)

    SoftwareType.cs:672–687

    Fittingly, this is also the efficiency trap of the game: a feature with CodeArt = 1 needs only programmers, 0 only artists, 0.5 both. Staffing a studio for your actual design means watching this number, because unused roles do no work at all.

    FinalQualityCalc — the equation that decides everything

    SoftwareAlpha.FinalQualityCalcprogress = ratio × codeProgress + (1 − ratio) × artProgress quality = ratio × codeQuality + (1 − ratio) × artQuality RealQuality = progress × quality × (2 − progress + quality) / 2

    SoftwareAlpha.cs:648–663; clamped to 0..1 at SoftwareProduct.cs:1149–1154

    ProgressQualityRealQualityNote
    1.001.001.000Perfect — reachable only with 3–4 design passes.
    1.000.750.656Full work, 2-pass design ceiling.
    1.000.500.375Full work, 1-pass ceiling — reviews will notice.
    0.901.000.945Releasing slightly early costs ~5 %.
    0.751.000.844Releasing at 75 % costs ~16 %.

    Progress is worth more than skill Going from 0.75 to 1.00 progress at fixed quality gains more than going from 3-star to 5-star staff at fixed progress. If you must ship early, ship with finished features and a lower design ceiling, not with unfinished work on an ambitious design.

    Per-submarket quality (the Quality[3] vector)

    SoftwareAlpha.GetMarketQuality — one value per submarketfor each feature f (not an OS feature): w = f.DevTime × max(1, f.Level) Quality[j] += f.FinalQuality × w × f.Submarkets[j] norm[j] += w × f.Submarkets[j] Quality[j] /= norm[j] // 0 if nothing feeds that submarket

    SoftwareAlpha.cs:949–980

    Note the asymmetry with spec points: spec points are weighted by level impact (1.5 / 3.0), while this quality average is weighted by max(1, Level) (1 / 2 / 3). A ★★ feature therefore pulls the submarket's average quality twice as hard as a ★ feature, but without the 3× spec-point bonus.

    03Bugs: the multiplier nobody budgets for

    Bugs never lower RealQuality. They attack the score at three other points: the review bug term, the refund rate, and the sales retention multiplier. They are also capped by a hard ceiling that depends on nothing but your dev time.

    The bug ceiling

    SoftwareWorkItem.GetMaximumBugsBugLimitFactor = 100 maxBugs = round( devTime × 100 × (devTime < 12 ? sqrt(min(1, devTime×0.75/12 + 0.25)) : 1) )

    SoftwareAlpha.cs:13; SoftwareWorkItem.cs:1019–1022

    For a normal project this is simply 100 bugs per dev month. A 40-month game has a 4,000-bug ceiling; a 10-month tool has 1,000 (× ~0.83 small-project scaling). Everything about bugs is measured against this number, not against an absolute count.

    Where bugs come from

    SoftwareAlpha.DoWork — bug generation (alpha only, code progress)Bugs += ΔcodeProgress × maxBugs × MapRange(skill, 0→1, 1.0→0.25) × (1 − 0.5 × SourceControlBoost) × BugRate

    SoftwareAlpha.cs:812

    Two multipliers matter. Skill: a 5-star programmer generates one quarter the bugs of a 0-star programmer for the same work. BugRate, set by how thoroughly you designed:

    Design completenessBugRateMaxBugFactorEffect

    Beta: how many bugs you can actually fix

    Maximum fixable bugs (SoftwareAlpha.MaxBugFix)MaxBugFix = Bugs × bestProgrammerSkill × (contract ? (skill+1)/2 : 1) × (Bugs > 100 ? 0.9 : 1)

    SoftwareAlpha.cs:603–636

    This is the decisive beta constraint Your best programmer on the team sets the fix ceiling. With a 3-star lead programmer you can only ever fix ~60 % of the bugs you generated — and every bug left on the build is scored as if you chose to ship it. Hiring one 5-star programmer before beta is worth more than ten 3-star ones.

    04Reviews: two completely different systems

    The game calls two unrelated things a "review", and almost every guide on the internet mixes them up. One is a development action with real mechanical consequences. The other is a news article with none.

    In-development peer reviewRelease press review
    WhenDuring design/alpha, before betaAfter release
    What you seePer-spec quality percentages + overall score /10Three critics with 1–5 stars and a quote
    Costs$100 per review written + dev timeFree
    Affects gameplay?Yes: unlocks iteration, cuts bug count, raises quality ceilingsNo: newspaper only
    Formula sourceReviewWork.cs, DesignDocument.FinishIterationFinalReviewGenerator, ArticleGenerator
    Myth: "high review stars sell more copies" Every consumer of FinalReviewGenerator.Review in the shipped assembly is the review window and the newspaper text. The star value is never written into the product, the company, followers, reputation or the sales loop. A 1-star and a 5-star release sell identically. What sells is the quality, hype and price that also caused those stars.

    The in-development review

    Starting a review queues review "work" that your team (or an external company, at $100 per review) grinds through. Each review covers one (role, spec) pair. Accuracy is what you are buying:

    Review accuracy (ReviewWork.EndReview)N = number of (role, spec) pairs being reviewed R = reviews completed optimal = max(10, ceil(50 × clamp01(Σ validReviewDevTime / 12))) accuracy = clamp01( (R − N) / (optimal × N − N) ) // each spec's reported score is perturbed by a Gaussian with σ = (1 − accuracy) × 0.2 // the displayed "overall score" is the plain mean of the per-spec scores × 10

    ReviewWork.cs:131–134, 226–300; ReviewWindow.cs:497 (ScoreString = round(score×100)/10)

    So: one pass per spec gives you a totally noisy report (σ = 0.2). Only at optimal × N reviews, i.e. the default slider at 10× the minimum, do you get a perfectly accurate report. Since the report costs $100 per review, accuracy is a straight money purchase.

    Reviewer sliderReviewsAccuracyCost (10 specs)Use it when
    1× (minimum)10 × specs~0.00$1,000+You only want the iteration / bug cut.
    5×50 × specs~0.44$5,000You want a rough read before continuing.

    Iteration: the strongest quality lever in the game

    After a review you may run one iteration. An iteration is a design pass that trades progress for a higher quality ceiling — the only way to exceed your original design's ceiling.

    DesignDocument.FinishIteration — per featurenum = clamp01(featureProgress − lastIterationProgress) × accuracy × (iterationProgress / iterationDevTime) Progress ×= 1 − 0.5 × num TargetQual += 0.5 × num / floor(sqrt(DevTime)) // clamped to 1.0

    DesignDocument.cs:801–854 (fully quoted in the appendix); iteration dev time normalised to 1 total at SoftwareWorkItem.cs:852–870

    What an iteration gives you

    • A higher target quality ceiling (up to 1.0)
    • Bugs reduced at beta entry — Bugs ×= 1 − (1 − 0.25ReviewScore) × 0.1 (max −10 %)
    • Re-opens alpha work on the features you reviewed

    What it costs

    • Up to half the progress on every reviewed feature
    • One "unit" of design work (normalised, not project-sized)
    • A finished review first — DidLastReviewIteration is only cleared by one
    • Unavailable once the project is in beta
    Accumulated review score → bug factor (SoftwareAlpha)ReviewScore += accuracy × codeArtProgress × (ReviewsDone / 8) reviewFactor = 1 − 0.25ReviewScore bugReviewFactor = reviewFactor × 0.1

    SoftwareAlpha.cs:222–236, 1370

    Because ReviewScore compounds with ReviewsDone/8, a single accurate review at near-full progress gives reviewFactor ≈ 0.25 → about a 2.5 % bug cut. It takes several reviews to approach the 10 % cap. The iteration's ceiling gain is worth far more than the bug cut.

    The release press review

    Seven fictional outlets score your product from the same 16 input values. Each outlet applies its own weight table; the game then shows the three whose opinions were most "interesting".

    FinalReviewGenerator.Reviewer.GetScoresoft = Πw>0 (1 − w + w × value) avg = Σ (w × value) / Σ w score = (soft + avg) / 2 // 0..1 stars = clamp(1 + round(score × 4), 1, 5)

    FinalReviewGenerator.cs:56–71, 508

    Note the structure: half of each critic's verdict is a weighted average of the terms it cares about, and the other half is a product of softened terms. Because 1 − w + w×v is ≥ v for v ≤ 1, weak values are pulled up — but a critic that weights several terms at 1.0 takes them raw, and the product half punishes you for every one of them.

    Worked example — why "everything above average" still scores badly Take a critic with two 1.0 weights (e.g. Quality Assistance on quality and progress) and a product that is decent at both: 0.5 and 0.5. The weighted average is 0.5, but the product half is 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25, so the final score is (0.25 + 0.5)/2 = 0.375 — two stars (1 + round(1.5)). Raise both to 0.7 and it becomes (0.49 + 0.7)/2 = 0.595 — still only three stars. Critics with multiple 1.0 weights need near-perfect values to reach four or five.

    The game's own verdict: GetReviewTargetScore

    This single expression is what decides whether the user reviews on your product page come out positive or negative. It is also the most compact summary in the codebase of "how good is this product", so it is worth reading as a checklist:

    SoftwareProduct.GetReviewTargetScoretarget = clamp01( Qmw // market-weighted quality × W(CreativityScore, 0.80) × W(PV_tech / PV_notech, 0.75) // how modern your tech is × W(PV_notech, 0.10) // size of the feature set × W(priceFactor, 0.50) × W(clamp01(SequelBonus), 0.50) × W(1 − bugs/maxBugs, 0.75) ) // W(v, w) = 1 − w + w·v

    SoftwareProduct.cs:2288–2298

    Read the weights as "how much this term can hurt you". Quality enters at full strength (it is the base value), bugs at 0.75, tech at 0.75, price at 0.5, sequel at 0.5, feature-set size at 0.10.     05Sales: how quality becomes money

    Once per in-game day the market runs one tick per product. The tick computes a demand, clamps it to the remaining addressable market, splits it physical/digital, removes refunds and books the money.

    Time convention used throughout: every rate below is divided by DaysPerMonth (default 1), so at default settings one tick is one month of market time. Values quoted "per month" are per tick.

    The demand equation

    MarketSimulation.SimulateMonth — demand for one product, one tickS = Σj=0..2 Qrel[j] × marketMix[j] S ×= MagicFactor (126,543) × GetPopularityFactor() // 0.9 + 0.1 × category Popularity × RealQuality × TimeFactor[month] // seasonality, 0.93 … 1.33 × PricePenalty × PriceChangeFact × (1 + addonDemand × (subscription ? 1.0 : 0.5)) × AddonQualityEffect × GetCreativityFactor(creativity, isPlayer) ÷ DaysPerMonth // player companies only: S ×= 1 − w + w × reputation // w = RecognitionSalesFactor (0.6–0.8 by difficulty) // followers are a guaranteed floor, not a multiplier: drawn = followers < 100 ? followers : round(followers × 0.1 / DPM) S = max(S, floor(drawn × PricePenalty × PriceChangeFact))

    MarketSimulation.cs:2632–2674

    Then the market caps it// normal software: S = min( max(0, Popularity × Population − unitsSoldSoFar), S ) // OS-specific software: S = osCoverage < unitsSoldSoFar ? 0 : min(osCoverage − unitsSoldSoFar, S)

    MarketSimulation.cs:2675–2704; Population = 145,613,576

    Note what is not in this equation: review stars, review percentages, the age of the product, or marketing spend beyond the awareness/followers it already produced. Age enters indirectly, through the market-quality normalisation and userbase decay.

    Quality share: your competitor is the market leader

    MarketSimulation.CacheQuality → SoftwareProduct.GetQualitymarketMax[j] = Σall released products in category Qraw[j] + min(Σ Qraw, AI hype contribution) Qrel[j] = marketMax[j] == 0 ? 1 : min(1, Qraw[j] / marketMax[j]) Qmw = Σj Qrel[j] × Submarkets[j]

    MarketSimulation.cs:1706–1737; SoftwareProduct.cs:1725–1745

    Why "just make a good game" is not enough Your quality is divided by the sum of every product currently selling in that category — including your own previous releases. Being twice as good as everyone else in a crowded category still leaves Qrel well under 1. What matters is your share of the category's total quality mass, which is why a small, focused release calendar outperforms flooding the market.

    Price: two different curves and one permanent scar

    MarketSimulation.PriceFact — the volume curvex = price / (2 × market) // market = ideal price × perceived value PriceFact = Interpolate(PriceFactEval, x) // 41 samples; peak 1.24 at x = 0.35 // i.e. maximum volume at 0.7 × the ideal price, exactly 1.0 at the ideal price // PricePenalty — a hard cliff used as a sales gate: r = price / market PricePenalty = r ≤ 1.75 ? 1 : mapRange(r, 1.75 → 2.0, 1 → 0)

    MarketSimulation.cs:2047–2061; 41-sample table at MarketSimulation.cs:195

    Because revenue is price × units and there is no per-unit cost on digital sales, the revenue optimum sits at about 0.9–1.0× the "market" value — the volume curve's extra 24 % at 0.7× does not make up for the 30 % price cut (at 0.9× revenue is still 1.008× the revenue at 1.0×). Above 1.75× PricePenalty starts cutting and at 2.0× you sell nothing at all.

    "Market" is not the category ideal — it is your product's value Every price check uses idealPrice × PerceivedValue, and perceived value is min(1, Σ feature spec-time / OptimalDevTime). A small product with 40 % of the optimal feature mass is compared against 0.4× the category's ideal price, so charging the full ideal price for it counts as 2.5× overpriced. Content and price must scale together: the more of the type's optimal feature mass you implement, the higher the price the market will bear.
    SoftwareProduct.ActuallyChangePrice — PriceChangeFactLowestPrice = min(LowestPrice, newPrice) PriceChangeFact = (LowestPrice / newPrice)newPrice / OriginalPrice × 3

    SoftwareProduct.cs:1753–1771

    PriceChangeFact is a ratchet, never a bonus Because LowestPrice is updated before the ratio is computed, the factor is exactly 1 at your historical low and permanently below 1 above it. Discounts earn no bonus — they merely avoid a penalty — and raising the price after launch is punished twice, because the exponent also grows with newPrice / OriginalPrice.

    Followers: the guaranteed floor

    Followers as guaranteed unitsdrawn = round(followers × 0.1 / DPM) // 10 % of the base per month followers −= drawn salesFloor = floor(drawn × PricePenalty × PriceChangeFact)

    MarketSimulation.cs:2652–2674

    Followers do not multiply sales — they replace them when organic demand is lower. A product with 50,000 followers sells at least ~5,000 units a month for ten months even if its quality is poor. This is also why pre-release hype is so valuable: it converts into guaranteed units at a fixed rate.

    Physical, digital, subscriptions and refunds

    Physical vs digital

    A 52-entry table indexed by year − 1970 and lerped by month: 100 % physical in 1970, ~90 % by 2000, ~59 % by 2010, 39 % by 2021 and frozen there. Physical units need printed stock, cost $2 per copy (×2 for hardware) and sell-outs lose the demand — it does not shift to digital.

    Refunds

    refundRate = min(1, loadIncidents/24 × 0.5 + min(0.75, bugs/maxBugs)²). Refunds are removed from the units you just sold and also shrink the userbase.

    Subscriptions

    Revenue is (Userbase + newSubs) × price / DPM every tick. The ideal price is only 8 % of the one-off price, and price penalties multiply churn.

    Add-ons & DLC

    Add-on demand feeds the parent formula as (1 + demand × 0.5) and grows the parent's userbase by (1 + 0.25 × demand). A good expansion is worth more to the base game than to itself.

    06Reputation, fans and marketing

    Reputation is the only "long game" stat: it multiplies every future product's market quality and sales, raises the follower ceiling, and is what reviewers mean by "everybody knows who you are".

    Reputation is derived from fans, not stored directly

    Company.ActuallyAddFans → reputationcap[cat] = Popularity × FanPopulation (9,100,848) pop = min(fans[cat] + leak, cap) // leak = 10 % of a sibling category's lead, 5 % cross-type x = clamp01(pop / cap) diff = hardware ? 0 : mapRange(IdealPrice × Popularity, 50 → 200, 0 → 1, clamp) reputation[cat] = lerp( _popScore(x), x², diff )

    The _popScore curve is concave — the first fans buy you reputation quickly, the last millions barely register. Softer in cheap categories (diff near 0 uses the curve), harsher in expensive ones (where reputation approaches x²).

    The −0.2 threshold A product only creates fans above 0.2 weighted quality — and can destroy fans below it, down to −1× sales. A cheap bad release is not neutral: it actively erodes the reputation you need for the next one.

    Awareness: the marketing channel

    SoftwareProduct.SimulateAwarenessawareness = max(0, awareness × (1 − lerp(0.15, 0.10, creativityAwareness) / DPM) + Marketing) Marketing = 0 // and it is normalised against the strongest product in the category: GetAwareness() = clamp01( awareness / maxAwarenessInCategory )

    09The playbook

    Everything above collapses into a small number of decisions. These are the ones the formulas actually reward.

    1. Design passes are cheap quality

    Three passes buy a 1.0 ceiling; four removes the variance. A pass is one full DevTime of design work, but design accrues at 2/3 per hour (twice the alpha rate) and is multiplied by the difficulty's design bonus (×1–3), so the whole four-pass design phase is usually a small fraction of alpha. Never skip design — one pass means a 0.5 ceiling, which caps the product below what any amount of alpha work can fix.

    2. Finish features, don't polish them

    FinalQualityCalc is multiplicative in progress. Shipping at 75 % progress costs ~16 % of final quality; shipping at 50 % costs ~38 %. A finished ★ feature beats a half-finished ★★ feature every time.

    3. One 5-star programmer before beta

    Bug fixing is capped at bestProgrammerSkill × bugs. Without a strong programmer you literally cannot clear the bug list, and bugs cost up to 75 % of your retention multiplier plus up to 50 % of your review score.

    4. Release in Q4

    November/December are 1.28× and 1.33×; August is 0.93×. Planning a project so it lands in Q4 is one of the highest-return decisions in the game.

    5. Price at 1.0× ideal, never above 1.75×

    Revenue peaks near the ideal price. Above 1.75× you hit the PricePenalty cliff and can zero out sales. Never discount early: the historical low becomes a permanent multiplier scar.

    6. Hype is a floor, not a multiplier

    Followers convert to guaranteed units at 10 % per month. 50,000 followers is ~5,000 units/month for ten months even for a bad product. Build followers before release, not after.

    7. Respect the quality-share denominator

    Your quality is divided by the category's total quality mass — including your own older products. Two good products in one category split each other's share. Either space releases or use a different category.

    8. Long tail beats launch spike

    Retention 72 (OS) keeps 46 % of the userbase after a year; Retention 12 (sports) keeps 1 %. Plan updates every Retention/2 months to reset decay, and prefer categories you can support for years.

    Five myths this source code kills

    MythReality
    "Review stars affect sales"No. Stars are cosmetic; the review is newspaper text.

    CHow this was produced

    1. Identified the runtime. The installation is a Unity 2018.4.36f1 Mono build, so the game logic lives in managed IL in Software Inc_Data\Managed\Assembly-CSharp.dll (5.5 MB, 3,279 types) rather than in IL2CPP metadata. REA's inspect-managed-artifact confirms the artefact identity: PE32 x86 CLI, runtime family unity-mono, assembly Assembly-CSharp v0.0.0.0, SHA-256 c877dbc5…7e58bd0, 5,553,152 bytes (evidence ev_4ce02ec1…645e2a, recorded in analysis/assembly-identity.json).
    2. Decompiled the assemblies. A small offline tool built against ICSharpCode.Decompiler 9.1 decompiled every top-level type of Assembly-CSharp.dll and Assembly-CSharp-firstpass.dll to readable C# (902 + 195 files), preserving field names and constants.
    3. Extracted the data. The game's numeric data ships as Unity TextAssets inside resources.assets. A minimal SerializedFile reader pulled all 202 text assets (software types, review weights, tutorials, names) out of the binary, and a TYD parser converted them to JSON.
    4. Traced the score chain. From SoftwareWorkItem and SoftwareAlpha (design/alpha/beta) through SoftwareProduct (quality, decay) to MarketSimulation (sales) and FinalReviewGenerator (press reviews), every multiplier was read from source rather than inferred.
    5. Cross-checked. The numbers in the tables are computed from the extracted assets by script, not typed by hand. Formulas marked derived are arithmetic that follows from the code but is not written as one expression in it.
    Limits of this document Everything is from a static reading of one build (Beta 1.8.42). Behaviours that depend on runtime state — the exact market mix, AI competitor quality, your save's difficulty — are described as formulas, not as fixed numbers. The game's Unity-side interpolation helper lives in a separate assembly and was recovered separately, so the tables that use it (price curve, creativity curve, quality labels) are exact.

    DGlossary

    Spec / spec feature

    The category-defining feature of a software type (e.g. "2D graphics"). Level 0, contributes 0.5× its dev time in points, and is the unit the review system reports on.

    Submarket

    One of the three axes every type scores on (Gameplay / Graphics / Story for games). Features distribute their points across all three.

    Spec point

    DevTime × level impact. The currency of quality.

    Target quality

    The hidden per-feature ceiling rolled when design ends. Alpha work cannot exceed it; only an iteration can raise it.

    RealQuality

    The single 0..1 number the product ships with. Not an average of features — a multiplicative function of progress and quality.

    BugScore

    Squared penalty for bugs relative to the dev-time ceiling. Feeds reviews and, via retention, sales.
